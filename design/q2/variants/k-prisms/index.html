The gap as height: CMAs rise and sink, quarter by quarter

Motivation. Height is an intuitive stand-in for size, and an oblique view keeps the real geography; the top colour can show either the gap size or its change against a year earlier.

Appeal. Ontario's CMAs as raised blocks that sink as the gap narrows; tilt the map to see it flat.

Cost. 3D distorts length and hides blocks behind others; values must be read in the card and Figure 2; a choropleth-like area effect remains.

This version does not use the agreed dumbbell baseline. It shares version F's data, outlook rules, card, Figure 2 and tables; only Figure 1a differs. Exact values are in the card, Figure 2 and Table 2.

Read this first: what the numbers measure

Gap, and its change

Gap = ODSP share − OW share of cases that are a single person without children, in percentage points (pp), for one CMA in one quarter. Change = gap now − gap a year (four quarters) earlier: positive = wider, negative = narrower.

Outlook

2026 Q2 – 2030 Q4, hatched. Each rule extends a CMA's gap mechanically from the data up to 2026 Q1. It is a projection, not a forecast: it knows nothing about future policy or the economy.

The three rules

Convergence: the gap keeps moving toward the middle CMA's gap at the rate seen in the past. No change: it stays at its 2026 Q1 value. 10-year trend: it follows its straight-line trend of the last 10 years.

How the rules were tested

Each rule was run from every past quarter that has 10 years of data before it, using only the data up to that quarter, and compared with what happened. The "likely range" is the middle 80% of those past misses.

Cases, small CMAs, pandemic

A case can include more than one person. Shares in small CMAs move more from quarter to quarter. Pandemic: March 2020 – May 2023 (2020 Q1 – 2023 Q1 in quarterly data).
