Motivation. The finding is a sequence: Ontario's two shares, how they moved, the same for every CMA, which program moved, how the gaps converged, and the exceptions. Scrolling tells it in that order with one set of dots, so the reader never has to re-learn a chart.

Appeal. The same dots move between scenes: two dots become two lines, the lines split into 720 dots that form two clouds, and each CMA's pair of dots collapses into its gap, line by line converging.

Cost. The most work of all versions; scroll-driven pages are harder to make accessible and to test, so every scene can also be reached with numbered buttons, the motion stops with reduced-motion settings, and the full explorer (version D) follows the story.

This version does not use the agreed dumbbell baseline, because a dumbbell shows two moments and only the size of the gap. The story shows which program moved and how the gaps converged; the ranking is kept in Figure 3 (start and end gaps) and Table 1.

Read this first: what the numbers measure

Share of cases

Single-person cases without children ÷ all cases × 100, for one program, one CMA, one quarter. It describes who is on the caseload, not how many single residents receive assistance.

Gap

ODSP share − OW share, in percentage points (pp), for one CMA. In the story it is the hatched band (Ontario) and, from scene 5, the height of each CMA's line.

Space between the clouds

The lowest ODSP share among the 15 CMAs minus the highest OW share in the same quarter. It compares the two groups of CMAs, so it is smaller than every single CMA's gap.

Narrowed by

Gap at the start quarter − gap at the end quarter (default 2003 Q1 → 2026 Q1). Positive means narrower; it does not mean narrower every quarter.

Cases, small CMAs, pandemic

A case can include more than one person. Shares in small CMAs move more from quarter to quarter, so hover shows the counts. Pandemic: March 2020 – May 2023 (2020 Q1 – 2023 Q1 in quarterly data).
